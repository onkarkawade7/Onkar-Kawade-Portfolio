01

The basics

Epilepsy is a disorder of recurrent, unprovoked seizures caused by abnormal, excessive, synchronous neuronal firing. Antiepileptic drugs suppress this abnormal firing without eliminating normal neuronal activity.

Definition · KD Tripathi

What is epilepsy?

A group of CNS disorders characterised by paroxysmal cerebral dysrhythmia, manifesting as brief episodes — seizures — of loss or disturbance of consciousness, with or without characteristic body movements, sensory or psychiatric phenomena.

The episodes are unpredictable and their frequency is highly variable. A seizure is a transient event; epilepsy is the tendency to have recurrent unprovoked seizures.

Clinical aim

Goals of therapy

  • Seizure freedom with the lowest effective dose of a single drug.
  • Protect function — minimise cognitive, behavioural and motor adverse effects.
  • Prevent injury and reduce psychosocial consequences of seizures.
  • Individualise therapy by seizure type, age, sex, comorbidity and drug interactions.

Not every seizure is epilepsy. A single tonic–clonic seizure in a person with no predisposing factor — no head injury, family history, neurological abnormality, abnormal EEG or brain scan — may not merit long-term antiepileptic therapy. Always look for a reversible cause first.

Spectrum

Broad-spectrum drugs

Useful across both focal and generalised seizures — but “broad” does not mean interchangeable.

  • Valproate — absence, myoclonic, tonic–clonic, focal.
  • Lamotrigine — focal and generalised, including absence.
  • Levetiracetam — focal and generalised; few interactions.
  • Topiramate — focal and generalised; broad but sedating.
  • Phenobarbitone, clonazepam, zonisamide, felbamate — broader coverage with notable limitations.
Spectrum

Narrow-spectrum drugs

Match the drug to the seizure type; some can worsen seizures they are not meant to treat.

  • Phenytoin, carbamazepine — focal and tonic–clonic; may worsen absence/myoclonic seizures.
  • Ethosuximide — absence seizures only.
  • Gabapentin, pregabalin — focal seizures; also neuropathic pain.
  • Vigabatrin, tiagabine, lacosamide, perampanel — focal seizures (selected uses).

Exam framing: classify by structure (Tripathi), then by mechanism, then by clinical spectrum. The seizure-type table later in these notes is the practical bridge between the three.

03

How they work

Seizures reflect an imbalance between excitation and inhibition. Antiepileptic drugs either dampen excitatory currents or strengthen inhibitory GABA signalling — usually in a use-dependent way, so normal activity is relatively spared.

Voltage-gated Na⁺ channel block

Prolongs the inactivated state of Na⁺ channels and reduces high-frequency repetitive firing. This is the commonest mechanism among first-line focal AEDs.

PhenytoinCarbamazepineLamotrigineValproateTopiramateLacosamideZonisamideRufinamide

GABA-A receptor facilitation

Increases Cl⁻ conductance and hyperpolarises neurons. Barbiturates increase channel duration; benzodiazepines increase channel frequency.

PhenobarbitonePrimidoneDiazepamLorazepamClonazepamClobazam

GABA metabolism & reuptake

Raises synaptic GABA by blocking its breakdown or its reuptake. Vigabatrin is irreversible; tiagabine targets the GAT-1 transporter.

VigabatrinTiagabineValproate

T-type Ca²⁺ channel block

Targets low-threshold T-type Ca²⁺ currents in thalamic relay neurons — the pacemaker of 3 Hz spike-and-wave discharges in absence epilepsy.

EthosuximideValproateZonisamide

α₂δ subunit of Ca²⁺ channels

Binds the α₂δ auxiliary subunit of voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels and reduces excitatory neurotransmitter release. Also useful in neuropathic pain.

GabapentinPregabalin

Glutamate & other targets

Reduce excitatory transmission at AMPA/NMDA receptors, modulate vesicular release, or inhibit carbonic anhydrase. These are mainly newer or reserve drugs.

TopiramateFelbamatePerampanelLevetiracetamAcetazolamide

Use-dependence is the key idea. Phenytoin and carbamazepine block Na⁺ channels more strongly when neurons fire rapidly, which is why they suppress seizure discharges but spare normal firing. It also explains why they are less useful in absence epilepsy, where the pathology is thalamic T-type Ca²⁺ pacemaking.

Drug profiles

The high-yield facts for each agent: mechanism, clinical uses, adverse effects and the interactions that show up in exams and on the wards. Tap a drug to expand it.

Antiepileptic drug profiles

01 Phenytoin Hydantoin · first-line focal & tonic–clonic · Na⁺ channel blocker

Mechanism

Use-dependent block of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels; prolongs the inactivated state and limits high-frequency repetitive firing. Also reduces synaptic transmission and post-tetanic potentiation.

Clinical uses

Generalised tonic–clonic and focal seizures; status epilepticus (IV phenytoin or fosphenytoin); trigeminal neuralgia; arrhythmias (historical). Not effective in absence seizures.

Adverse effects

  • Gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, acne, coarsening of facial features.
  • Ataxia, nystagmus, dysarthria, sedation, cognitive slowing.
  • Megaloblastic anaemia (folate deficiency), osteomalacia (vitamin D deficiency).
  • Hypersensitivity rash, SJS/TEN, DRESS; fetal hydantoin syndrome.
  • IV: hypotension, arrhythmias, thrombophlebitis, purple glove syndrome.

Kinetics & interactions

Slow, variable oral absorption; ~90% protein-bound; hepatic metabolism is saturable (zero-order) at therapeutic doses, so small increments can cause toxicity. Potent enzyme inducer; displaces warfarin and steroids; valproate inhibits its metabolism.

Therapeutic range 10–20 µg/mL Zero-order kinetics Gum hyperplasia Teratogenic
02 Fosphenytoin Hydantoin prodrug · IV/IM · safer local profile

Mechanism

Water-soluble phosphate prodrug of phenytoin, rapidly hydrolysed by phosphatases to phenytoin. Same pharmacological action as phenytoin.

Clinical uses

Status epilepticus and other situations needing parenteral phenytoin. Can be given IM when IV access is difficult. Doses are expressed as phenytoin equivalents (PE).

Advantages

Less local irritation and phlebitis than IV phenytoin; can be infused faster (up to 150 mg PE/min) with cardiac monitoring. No propylene glycol or ethanol vehicle.

Adverse effects

Similar systemic toxicity to phenytoin; hypotension and arrhythmias with rapid infusion; paraesthesia, pruritus, rash; rare anaphylaxis. Still requires cardiac monitoring.

Prodrug Phenytoin equivalents Status epilepticus
03 Carbamazepine Iminostilbene · DOC complex partial · trigeminal neuralgia

Mechanism

Blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels in a use-dependent manner and reduces polysynaptic responses. Metabolised to an active epoxide; induces its own metabolism (autoinduction).

Clinical uses

Drug of choice for complex partial (temporal lobe) seizures and secondarily generalised tonic–clonic seizures; generalised tonic–clonic; trigeminal neuralgia; bipolar disorder; some neuropathic pain.

Adverse effects

  • Diplopia, ataxia, dizziness, sedation — common and dose-related.
  • Hyponatraemia / SIADH, especially in the elderly.
  • Aplastic anaemia, agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia — rare but serious.
  • Hepatotoxicity; rash, SJS/TEN (HLA-B*1502 in Asian populations), DRESS.
  • Neural tube defects; reduces efficacy of oral contraceptives.

Kinetics & interactions

Hepatic metabolism; autoinduction shortens its half-life over 3–6 weeks. Potent CYP inducer (3A4, 2C9): lowers levels of oral contraceptives, warfarin, steroids, other AEDs. Valproate raises the active epoxide.

DOC complex partial Hyponatraemia HLA-B*1502 Autoinduction
04 Oxcarbazepine Iminostilbene · focal seizures · fewer interactions than carbamazepine

Mechanism

Prodrug rapidly reduced to the active monohydroxy derivative (MHD), which blocks Na⁺ channels. Similar antiseizure spectrum to carbamazepine.

Clinical uses

Focal seizures with or without secondary generalisation; trigeminal neuralgia; adjunct in some generalised seizures.

Adverse effects

Hyponatraemia (more common than with carbamazepine), dizziness, diplopia, ataxia, rash, headache, nausea. Cross-hypersensitivity with carbamazepine is possible.

Pearls

Less enzyme induction than carbamazepine and fewer drug interactions, but it can still reduce oral contraceptive efficacy. Monitor sodium in older adults.

Focal seizures Hyponatraemia MHD
05 Sodium valproate Aliphatic carboxylic acid · broad-spectrum · absence, myoclonic, tonic–clonic

Mechanism

Multiple actions: Na⁺ channel block, T-type Ca²⁺ channel inhibition, increased GABA (inhibits GABA transaminase and succinic semialdehyde dehydrogenase). Broad-spectrum.

Clinical uses

Drug of choice for generalised absence and myoclonic seizures; also generalised tonic–clonic and focal seizures; bipolar disorder; migraine prophylaxis. Often used when several seizure types coexist.

Adverse effects

  • Hepatotoxicity — highest risk in children under 2 and with polytherapy.
  • Pancreatitis, hyperammonaemia, thrombocytopenia.
  • Weight gain, tremor, alopecia, polycystic ovaries.
  • Neural tube defects and developmental harm — avoid in pregnancy if possible.

Kinetics & interactions

Highly protein-bound; hepatic metabolism. Inhibits CYP2C9 and UGT, and inhibits epoxide hydrolase. Raises lamotrigine levels markedly (slow titration, rash risk); displaces phenytoin; raises carbamazepine epoxide.

Broad spectrum Hepatotoxic Teratogenic Pancreatitis
06 Ethosuximide Succinimide · DOC absence seizures · T-type Ca²⁺ blocker

Mechanism

Selectively inhibits low-threshold T-type Ca²⁺ channels in thalamic relay neurons, suppressing the 3 Hz spike-and-wave discharge of absence epilepsy.

Clinical uses

Drug of choice for simple absence seizures, especially when tonic–clonic seizures are absent. Ineffective for focal seizures and generalised tonic–clonic seizures.

Adverse effects

Gastrointestinal upset, nausea, anorexia; sedation, ataxia, headache; rash; rare blood dyscrasias and SLE-like syndrome. Psychiatric disturbance can occur.

Pearls

Long half-life allows once- or twice-daily dosing. If both absence and tonic–clonic seizures coexist, valproate is usually preferred because ethosuximide will not cover the tonic–clonic component.

DOC absence T-type Ca²⁺ Paediatric use
07 Phenobarbitone Barbiturate · neonatal seizures · GABA-A facilitator

Mechanism

Binds a barbiturate site on the GABA-A receptor–Cl⁻ channel complex and increases the duration of channel opening, enhancing inhibitory neurotransmission. At high doses, also reduces glutamate-mediated excitation.

Clinical uses

Neonatal seizures; generalised tonic–clonic and focal seizures; status epilepticus when other agents are unavailable; alternatives in resource-limited settings.

Adverse effects

Sedation, cognitive impairment, hyperactivity and behavioural problems in children; rash; megaloblastic anaemia, osteomalacia; tolerance and dependence; respiratory depression in overdose. Enzyme inducer.

Pearls

Cheap and effective, but its sedative and cognitive burden makes it a second-line choice in most modern protocols. Abrupt withdrawal can precipitate seizures.

Neonatal seizures Sedation Enzyme inducer
08 Primidone Deoxybarbiturate · prodrug of phenobarbitone

Mechanism

Metabolised to phenobarbitone (major active metabolite) and phenylethylmalonamide. Shares the GABA-A facilitating action of barbiturates.

Clinical uses

Generalised tonic–clonic and focal seizures; essential tremor (selected use). Largely superseded by better-tolerated agents.

Adverse effects

Sedation, dizziness, ataxia, nausea, rash; behavioural changes; megaloblastic anaemia, osteomalacia; enzyme induction. Tolerance and dependence occur.

Pearls

Start low and titrate slowly because the first dose can be markedly sedating. Like phenobarbitone, it is an enzyme inducer with many interactions.

Prodrug Phenobarbitone Sedation
09 Benzodiazepines Diazepam · lorazepam · clonazepam · clobazam · GABA-A facilitators

Mechanism

Bind the benzodiazepine site on GABA-A receptors and increase the frequency of Cl⁻ channel opening. They do not open the channel by themselves — endogenous GABA is required.

Clinical uses

Lorazepam IV is first-line for status epilepticus; diazepam IV/rectal for status and febrile seizures; clonazepam for myoclonic and absence seizures; clobazam as adjunct in focal and generalised epilepsies, including Lennox–Gastaut.

Adverse effects

Sedation, ataxia, dizziness, anterograde amnesia, muscle weakness; respiratory depression with rapid IV use; tolerance, dependence and withdrawal seizures with long-term use.

Pearls

Reserve long-term benzodiazepines for patients who need them; tolerance to the antiseizure effect develops. Flumazenil is the antagonist. Rectal diazepam and intranasal/buccal midazolam are practical out-of-hospital rescue options.

Status epilepticus Tolerance Respiratory depression
10 Lamotrigine Phenyltriazine · broad-spectrum · slow titration is essential

Mechanism

Use-dependent Na⁺ channel block; also reduces glutamate release. Broad-spectrum activity against focal and generalised seizures.

Clinical uses

Focal seizures, generalised tonic–clonic, absence and myoclonic seizures; Lennox–Gastaut; bipolar depression maintenance. Often chosen when cognitive tolerability matters.

Adverse effects

Rash is the signature concern — can progress to SJS/TEN, especially with rapid titration or concurrent valproate. Also insomnia, headache, dizziness, diplopia, ataxia, nausea; rare haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis.

Kinetics & interactions

Half-life ~25–30 h as monotherapy, prolonged to ~60 h by valproate, shortened to ~12–15 h by enzyme inducers. Start low and go slow; halve the dose when adding valproate. Oral contraceptives can lower lamotrigine levels.

Broad spectrum Rash / SJS Slow titration
11 Levetiracetam Pyrrolidone derivative · broad-spectrum · minimal interactions

Mechanism

Binds the synaptic vesicle protein SV2A and modulates neurotransmitter release. The exact downstream antiseizure mechanism is still debated.

Clinical uses

Focal seizures with or without secondary generalisation; generalised tonic–clonic and myoclonic seizures; adjunct in status epilepticus; often preferred in pregnancy, liver disease and the elderly because of its favourable kinetics.

Adverse effects

Somnolence, fatigue, dizziness; behavioural effects — irritability, aggression, anxiety, psychosis, especially in children and those with prior psychiatric history. Rare rash/SJS.

Kinetics & interactions

Rapid oral absorption; minimal protein binding; largely renal excretion; half-life 6–8 h. Few clinically significant drug interactions — a major advantage in polytherapy. Dose-adjust in renal impairment.

SV2A Renal excretion Behavioural effects
12 Gabapentin & pregabalin Cyclic GABA analogues · α₂δ ligands · focal seizures & neuropathic pain

Mechanism

Bind the α₂δ subunit of voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels and reduce release of excitatory neurotransmitters. They are not true GABA agonists despite the name.

Clinical uses

Adjunctive therapy for focal seizures; neuropathic pain (diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia); generalised anxiety disorder (pregabalin); gabapentin is also used for restless legs and pruritus.

Adverse effects

Dizziness, somnolence, ataxia, fatigue, peripheral oedema, weight gain. Pregabalin can cause euphoria and has misuse potential; both can cause withdrawal seizures if stopped abruptly. Rare angioedema and respiratory depression with opioids.

Kinetics & pearls

Renally excreted; gabapentin absorption is saturable (dose-dependent bioavailability), while pregabalin has ~90% bioavailability. Dose-adjust in renal impairment. Not effective for absence or myoclonic seizures.

α₂δ ligand Neuropathic pain Renal dosing
13 Topiramate Fructose derivative · broad-spectrum · multiple mechanisms

Mechanism

Blocks Na⁺ channels, antagonises AMPA/kainate glutamate receptors, potentiates GABA-A activity, and inhibits carbonic anhydrase. A true multi-target drug.

Clinical uses

Focal and generalised tonic–clonic seizures; Lennox–Gastaut; adjunct in absence/myoclonic epilepsy; migraine prophylaxis. Sometimes used for weight management and bipolar disorder.

Adverse effects

  • Cognitive slowing and word-finding difficulty — often limiting.
  • Paraesthesias, somnolence, dizziness, fatigue.
  • Nephrolithiasis, metabolic acidosis, oligohidrosis in children.
  • Acute angle-closure glaucoma; weight loss; rare hepatic failure.

Pearls

Start low and titrate slowly to limit cognitive effects. Avoid in patients with a history of kidney stones. Enzyme inducer at higher doses; can reduce oral contraceptive efficacy at ≥200 mg/day.

Broad spectrum Kidney stones Word-finding Weight loss
14 Zonisamide Benzisoxazole · focal & generalised · carbonic anhydrase inhibitor

Mechanism

Blocks Na⁺ channels and T-type Ca²⁺ channels; inhibits carbonic anhydrase. Broad-spectrum but mainly used as adjunct.

Clinical uses

Adjunct for focal seizures, generalised tonic–clonic, myoclonic and absence seizures; selected cases of Lennox–Gastaut and infantile spasms.

Adverse effects

Somnolence, dizziness, ataxia, cognitive slowing; nephrolithiasis, metabolic acidosis, oligohidrosis; rash/SJS; weight loss; rare aplastic anaemia and renal failure.

Pearls

Long half-life (~60 h) allows once-daily dosing. Avoid in sulfonamide hypersensitivity and kidney stone disease. Monitor bicarbonate with long-term use.

Long half-life Kidney stones Sulfonamide
15 Vigabatrin & tiagabine GABAergic · visual field risk · focal seizures

Mechanism

Vigabatrin irreversibly inhibits GABA transaminase, raising GABA levels. Tiagabine inhibits the GAT-1 GABA reuptake transporter, prolonging GABA action.

Clinical uses

Vigabatrin: infantile spasms (West syndrome), adjunct in focal seizures, selected tuberous sclerosis cases. Tiagabine: adjunct for focal seizures.

Adverse effects

Vigabatrin: irreversible visual field constriction (mandatory visual monitoring), sedation, weight gain, psychosis, MRI abnormalities in infants. Tiagabine: dizziness, asthenia, nervousness, myoclonus, paradoxical seizures.

Pearls

Vigabatrin’s visual toxicity restricts it to serious indications such as infantile spasms. Tiagabine is a second-line adjunct and is not used for generalised epilepsies.

Visual field loss GABA transaminase GAT-1
16 Lacosamide, perampanel & rufinamide Newer adjuncts · focal seizures · special syndromes

Mechanisms

Lacosamide enhances slow inactivation of Na⁺ channels. Perampanel is a selective AMPA receptor antagonist. Rufinamide prolongs Na⁺ channel inactivation and is used in Lennox–Gastaut.

Clinical uses

Lacosamide and perampanel: adjunct for focal seizures, with perampanel also approved for generalised tonic–clonic seizures. Rufinamide: adjunct for Lennox–Gastaut syndrome.

Adverse effects

Lacosamide: dizziness, ataxia, diplopia, headache, PR prolongation (caution in cardiac disease). Perampanel: dizziness, somnolence, falls, psychiatric/behavioural effects. Rufinamide: somnolence, vomiting, headache, rash, QT shortening.

Pearls

These are generally add-on drugs for refractory epilepsy. Perampanel is taken at bedtime because of sedation; its long half-life allows once-daily dosing. Rufinamide must be titrated slowly.

Lacosamide · PR interval Perampanel · AMPA Rufinamide · LGS
17 Felbamate & acetazolamide Reserve drug · adjunct · serious toxicity

Mechanism

Felbamate antagonises NMDA/glycine receptors and potentiates GABA; acetazolamide inhibits carbonic anhydrase, producing mild metabolic acidosis that reduces neuronal excitability.

Clinical uses

Felbamate: refractory focal seizures and Lennox–Gastaut, reserved because of toxicity. Acetazolamide: adjunct in absence seizures and catamenial epilepsy; also used in glaucoma and altitude sickness.

Adverse effects

Felbamate: aplastic anaemia and hepatic failure — mandatory monitoring. Acetazolamide: paraesthesia, somnolence, metabolic acidosis, hypokalaemia, kidney stones; tolerance to the antiseizure effect develops.

Pearls

Felbamate is a drug of last resort. Acetazolamide is not a first-line maintenance AED; its effect wanes with continued use.

Aplastic anaemia Reserve Carbonic anhydrase

Never stop an AED abruptly. Abrupt withdrawal — especially of phenobarbitone, benzodiazepines or valproate — can precipitate status epilepticus. Taper over weeks to months under supervision.

Exam pearl

Absence vs focal

Ethosuximide treats only absence. Carbamazepine and phenytoin treat focal seizures but can worsen absence and myoclonic epilepsies. Always classify the seizure before choosing.

Exam pearl

Two seizure types

When absence and tonic–clonic seizures coexist, valproate is the practical single agent. Lamotrigine or levetiracetam are alternatives when valproate is unsuitable.

Exam pearl

Trigeminal neuralgia

Carbamazepine is first-line and dramatically effective. Oxcarbazepine is a better-tolerated alternative; baclofen and gabapentin are add-ons.

06

Status epilepticus

A neurological emergency: continuous seizure activity for ≥5 minutes (the practical definition; older texts use 30 minutes), or recurrent seizures without recovery of consciousness. Time is brain — treat while you investigate.

Do not wait for a diagnosis before treating. Secure the airway, give oxygen, check glucose, establish IV access, and start a benzodiazepine. Look for reversible causes in parallel: hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, infection, stroke, trauma, eclampsia, drug toxicity or withdrawal.

  1. 0–5

    Stabilise & investigate

    Airway, breathing, circulation; high-flow oxygen; cardiac and SpO₂ monitoring; IV access. Check capillary glucose — give thiamine 100 mg IV before glucose if deficiency is suspected. Send electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, ABG, toxicology and relevant imaging.

    ABC first
  2. 5–20

    First-line benzodiazepine

    Lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV (max 4 mg) over 2 minutes, or diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg IV (max 10 mg) slowly. Repeat once after 5–10 minutes if the seizure continues. Without IV access: rectal diazepam, IM midazolam, or intranasal/buccal midazolam.

    Monitor respiration
  3. 20–40

    Second-line long-acting AED

    Fosphenytoin 15–20 mg PE/kg IV (max 1500 mg PE) or phenytoin 15–20 mg/kg IV at ≤50 mg/min with ECG and BP monitoring. Alternative: phenobarbitone 15–20 mg/kg IV at ≤100 mg/min. Levetiracetam or valproate are used as alternatives in some protocols.

    Cardiac monitoring
  4. 40+

    Refractory status

    Move to ICU. Midazolam infusion (bolus then continuous infusion), propofol, or thiopentone with intubation and mechanical ventilation. Continuous EEG monitoring is ideal; titrate to burst suppression or seizure control.

    ICU + EEG
  5. Cause

    Treat the precipitant

    Correct hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia and hypocalcaemia; treat meningitis/encephalitis; image for stroke or haemorrhage; manage eclampsia with magnesium sulfate; identify drug toxicity or withdrawal. Prevention of recurrence matters as much as stopping the seizure.

    Look for the why
07

Principles of therapy

Good epilepsy care is more than picking a drug. Start simple, titrate carefully, monitor what matters, and treat the patient — not just the EEG.

Step 1

Monotherapy first

Begin with a single appropriate AED at a low dose, then titrate to the lowest effective dose. Monotherapy improves adherence and limits interactions. Add a second drug only after adequate trials.

Step 2

Titrate slowly

Rapid titration increases rash (lamotrigine, carbamazepine), sedation and cognitive effects. Start low, go slow — especially in the elderly and in hepatic or renal impairment.

Step 3

Monitor selectively

Plasma levels are most useful for phenytoin (zero-order kinetics), carbamazepine, valproate and phenobarbitone. Monitor CBC, LFTs, sodium, calcium, vitamin D and visual fields where relevant.

Step 4

Withdraw gradually

After a suitable seizure-free period (often 2–5 years, depending on syndrome), taper slowly over weeks to months. Abrupt withdrawal can precipitate status epilepticus.

Step 5

Keep it simple

Use the fewest daily doses that work. A seizure diary, pill box and clear written instructions improve adherence more than complex regimens.

Step 6

Counsel the whole life

Discuss sleep deprivation, alcohol, driving, swimming, heights, pregnancy planning, contraception and bone health. Many seizure recurrences are triggered by lifestyle factors.

Adverse effects & interactions

Every AED has a signature toxicity. Learn the one or two effects that define each drug — those are what examinations and prescriptions test.

Interaction pattern

Enzyme inducers

Phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbitone, primidone and high-dose topiramate lower levels of oral contraceptives, warfarin, corticosteroids, vitamin D and many other AEDs. Expect contraceptive failure and drug withdrawal seizures.

Interaction pattern

Valproate inhibition

Valproate inhibits CYP2C9 and UGT, and blocks epoxide hydrolase. It raises lamotrigine (rash risk), displaces phenytoin, and raises carbamazepine epoxide. It is the classic “victim and perpetrator” AED.

Interaction pattern

Additive toxicity

Benzodiazepines plus opioids or alcohol cause respiratory depression. Carbamazepine plus macrolides, azoles or isoniazid can cause toxicity. Lamotrigine plus valproate increases rash risk. Always reconcile the full medication list.

09

Quick review

One-line associations, a mnemonic, and a few self-check questions. Use these for spaced repetition rather than last-minute cramming.

01

Phenytoin

Gum hyperplasia, hirsutism, ataxia, osteomalacia, zero-order kinetics. Therapeutic range 10–20 µg/mL.

02

Carbamazepine

DOC complex partial and trigeminal neuralgia; hyponatraemia, aplastic anaemia, HLA-B*1502, autoinduction.

03

Valproate

Broad-spectrum; DOC absence and myoclonic; hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, weight gain, neural tube defects.

04

Ethosuximide

Pure absence seizures; T-type Ca²⁺ channel blocker; no coverage of tonic–clonic seizures.

05

Lamotrigine

Broad-spectrum; rash/SJS; slow titration; valproate doubles its half-life — halve the dose.

06

Levetiracetam

SV2A binder; broad-spectrum; behavioural effects; renal excretion; minimal interactions.

07

Topiramate

Kidney stones, metabolic acidosis, word-finding difficulty, weight loss, glaucoma.

08

Vigabatrin

Irreversible GABA transaminase inhibitor; infantile spasms; irreversible visual field constriction.

09

Status epilepticus

ABC → IV lorazepam (or diazepam) → fosphenytoin/phenytoin or phenobarbitone → ICU/midazolam/propofol.

10

Pregnancy

Avoid valproate; prefer lamotrigine or levetiracetam; folic acid 5 mg/day; monotherapy at the lowest effective dose.

Mnemonic

VALPROATE

  • V — Vomiting, weight gain
  • A — Alopecia
  • L — Liver toxicity
  • P — Pancreatitis
  • R — Reproductive harm (neural tube defects)
  • O — Osteoporosis risk
  • A — Ammonia elevation
  • T — Tremor, thrombocytopenia
  • E — Enzyme inhibition (raises lamotrigine)
Self-check

Test yourself

DOC for pure absence seizures?

Ethosuximide. Valproate is preferred when absence coexists with tonic–clonic or myoclonic seizures.

Which AED causes gum hyperplasia and hirsutism?

Phenytoin. Also causes ataxia, nystagmus, megaloblastic anaemia and osteomalacia.

First-line drug for status epilepticus?

IV lorazepam (alternative IV diazepam), followed by a long-acting AED such as fosphenytoin/phenytoin or phenobarbitone.

Which AED causes irreversible visual field loss?

Vigabatrin. Visual field testing is mandatory; use is restricted to serious indications such as infantile spasms.

Which two AEDs are associated with kidney stones?

Topiramate and zonisamide — both inhibit carbonic anhydrase and cause metabolic acidosis.

Which AED binds SV2A and has minimal interactions?

Levetiracetam. It is renally excreted and is a useful option in liver disease and polytherapy.

Final revision rule: for every drug, be able to state its mechanism, its defining adverse effect, its key interaction, and the seizure type it treats. That four-point framework covers most exam questions.
